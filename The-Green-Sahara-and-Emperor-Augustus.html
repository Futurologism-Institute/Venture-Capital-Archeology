# Hypothesis: Hydrocarbons, a Green Sahara, and the Deep Ancestry of Southern Burkina Faso ## Statement of the hypothesis The Sahara, once a landscape of lakes, rivers, grasslands, and forest, may hold undiscovered archaeological and hydrocarbon resources. Southern Burkina Faso, where Saharan populations plausibly migrated as the desert expanded, may preserve a deep archaeological record that Western-led research has barely examined. The Kassena people of the Sud-Centre region may descend in part from those Saharan ancestors. ## The Green Sahara Between roughly 14,500 and 5,000 years ago, during the African Humid Period, the Sahara held large lakes, rivers, and herds of cattle, giraffes, and hippos. The people who lived there recorded this world in rock art at Tassili n'Ajjer, the Acacus, and the Cave of Swimmers. Saharan communities such as the Tuareg, Tubu, and others preserved memory of a greener land in oral tradition and in the art on their own cliff walls. Western science formalized the "Green Sahara" only in the twentieth century, and these Saharan peoples and their art were part of what made that recognition possible. The finds at Gobero in Niger, with its Holocene burials beside an ancient lake, show how much remains buried. ## The oil hypothesis Oil in the Sahara is established fact. Algeria's Hassi Messaoud and Libya's Sirte Basin are major fields, and Chad and Niger produce too. Those fields exist because ancient seas and lakes deposited organic-rich sediments that later became source rock. The same ancient waterways that sustained the Green Sahara are evidence of the basins that could hold more. For Burkina Faso, the hypothesis is more speculative. Most of southern Burkina sits on Birimian greenstone rock, which is rich in gold but poor in oil prospects. The stronger candidate is the Volta Basin, which stretches across eastern and southern Burkina into Ghana, and the Taoudeni Basin to the north. Both have drawn limited exploration interest but no commercial discovery. This hypothesis would need seismic surveys and test drilling to confirm. ## The ruins of Loropeni Loropeni, in Poni Province, is Burkina Faso's first UNESCO World Heritage Site (2009). It is a stone enclosure of about 11,000 square meters with walls up to six meters high. It was built by the ancestors of peoples in the Lobi region and is tied to the trans-Saharan gold trade. Its occupation probably spans many centuries, and it is only one of a larger group of stone-walled sites in the region, most of them never excavated. I hypothesize that Loropeni is part of a much larger network of settlements linking the savanna to Saharan trade routes, and that systematic excavation would reveal older layers than currently known. ## The Kassena and Saharan ancestry The Kassena, a Gur-speaking people living around Po, Tiébélé, and across the Ghana border, are known for their painted compounds and their royal court at Tiébélé. As the Sahara dried, populations moved south, and the Kassena may descend from some of them. This is plausible and archaeologically testable. The link to Upper and Lower Egypt is a stronger and more contested claim, and it is where the evidence is thinnest. Early Saharan cattle pastoralists, such as those at Nabta Playa, shared cultural traits with Predynastic Egypt, so a common Saharan-Nilotic cultural background is credible. But Gur languages belong to the Niger-Congo family while ancient Egyptian belongs to the Afroasiatic family, and no genetic or archaeological study has yet connected the Kassena to Egypt specifically. Ancient DNA work and excavation in Burkina Faso could settle this either way. ## The case for excavation Western archaeology has worked in the Sahara, but unevenly, and southern Burkina Faso has received little attention. I propose large-scale excavation, ideally led by or in partnership with African institutions and with Kassena and Lobi communities, including: - Stratigraphic digs at Loropeni and neighboring walled sites - Surveys of dry lakebeds and rock-art zones in the Sahara - Ancient DNA and linguistic studies of Sahelian populations - Geological surveys of the Volta and Taoudeni basins ## King Narmer Narmer ruled around 3100 BCE and is traditionally credited with unifying Upper and Lower Egypt, the start of Egyptian dynastic history. He came from Upper Egypt, with a base around Hierakonpolis and Abydos, where his tomb lies at Umm el-Qa'ab. The Narmer Palette, found at Hierakonpolis in 1898, shows him wearing the white crown of Upper Egypt on one side and the red crown of Lower Egypt on the other, striking down an enemy. Some scholars identify him with Menes, the legendary first king in later king lists, though this is debated. Most Egyptologists now see unification as a gradual process over generations rather than one conquest. Narmer's serekh (royal name symbol) has been found as far as southern Canaan, showing Egypt's early reach. Egypt's origins were themselves shaped by the drying Sahara: people displaced from the desert settled along the Nile, helping build Egyptian civilization. That shared Saharan origin is the best foundation for the hypothesis that peoples to the south and east may be distant cousins. **Where the evidence stands:** the Green Sahara, Saharan oil, Loropeni, and Narmer are well documented. The Volta Basin oil, the Kassena's Saharan origins, and any Egyptian link are open hypotheses that excavation and survey could test.
